Board Governance & Fiduciary Risk

Cyber Risk is Personal Liability

Cybersecurity is a non-delegable board duty. Incomplete controls expose directors to personal regulatory penalties, shareholder lawsuits, and voided insurance coverage.

Obligations That Apply to Every Australian Business

These duties do not depend on your industry, your regulator, or whether you hold a licence.

Corporations Act 2001

Sections 180 & 181

Directors must exercise care, diligence, and good faith. Accepting management assurances about cyber posture without independent technical verification is not diligence. Where controls are neither evidenced nor tested, a board has no factual basis for the assurances it has been giving itself.

Privacy Act 1988

Privacy & NDB Scheme

Civil penalties exceeding $50 million apply for serious or repeated interference with privacy. Businesses over $3 million turnover are covered, and health service providers are covered regardless of turnover. Failing to take reasonable steps to secure personal information is itself the breach, separate from whatever the attacker does with it.

Cyber Security Act 2024

Mandatory Ransomware Payment Reporting

Since 30 May 2025, any business carrying on business in Australia with turnover above $3 million must report a ransomware or cyber extortion payment to government within 72 hours. The obligation captures payments made overseas and payments made by a third party on your behalf. Not-for-profits are not exempt. Late reporting attracts a civil penalty.

There is no grace period and no discretion. If you pay, you disclose, and that disclosure becomes a record of the incident and the controls that failed to prevent it.

Contractual Flow-Down

Customers, Insurers, and Acquirers

The fastest-growing source of security obligation is not a regulator. It is your largest client's supplier security schedule, your insurer's renewal questionnaire, and the diligence checklist in your next transaction. These are contractual commitments. Signing one you cannot evidence is a misrepresentation with commercial consequences, whether or not any regulator ever looks at it.

Obligations That Depend on Your Sector

Select the regime your business operates under..

Financial Services

AFS Licensees & APRA-Regulated Entities

AFS licensees must maintain adequate risk management systems and adequate technological and human resources under section 912A of the Corporations Act. APRA-regulated banks, insurers, and superannuation funds carry additional obligations under CPS 234 and CPS 230, with boards held directly accountable for operational resilience and third-party risk.

Legal, Accounting & Property

AML/CTF Tranche 2

Since 1 July 2026, AUSTRAC obligations extend to lawyers, conveyancers, accountants, real estate professionals, trust and company service providers, and dealers in precious metals and stones. Roughly 80,000 to 100,000 businesses were captured for the first time. These firms must now collect and retain customer identification, beneficial ownership, and source of funds records for seven years. Passports. Driver's licences. Bank details. Most have added the obligation without adding a single control to protect it. Penalties reach $33 million for a body corporate.

Healthcare & Aged Care

Privacy Act & My Health Records

Health service providers are covered by the Privacy Act regardless of turnover, so the small business exemption does not apply. My Health Records adds a further layer of obligation and its own penalty regime. Health has been consistently among the most-notified sectors under the notifiable data breach scheme since it commenced.

Structural Exposure in Traditional Defense

■

Insurance Policy Voidance

Control attestations, such as claiming full MFA coverage without verified, continuously monitored enforcement, create material misrepresentation. In the event of a breach, insurers routinely deny coverage for gaps that were attested but never evidenced.

■

Legacy SOC & Alert Noise

Traditional Security Operations Centers (SOCs) generate high alert volume but provide low structural defense. Reactive monitoring creates an illusion of security, leaving boards legally exposed when alerts are missed or ignored.

■

AI Threat Velocity

AI-driven social engineering, automated credential harvesting, and deepfake authorised push payments bypass legacy perimeter defenses in seconds. Manual review processes can no longer meet the legally required standard of care.

■

Personal & Derivative Liability

D&O insurance policies increasingly exclude losses resulting from gross cyber negligence. Directors risk personal assets, disqualification, and shareholder derivative suits when security postures fail to meet statutory standards.

Technical Implications for the Board

Bridging statutory expectations with underlying technical realities

Essential Eight ML0

Statutory Negligence & Un-audited Blind Spots

Under Section 180 of the Corporations Act, a director relying on the business judgment rule needs to show they informed themselves about the subject matter to the extent they reasonably believed appropriate. Sitting at Essential Eight ML0, undocumented and unverified, is difficult to reconcile with that standard.

Self-Assessment Fallacy

Paper Compliance vs. Forensic Reality

Standards like CPS 234 and SOCI require technical controls to actively function in practice, not just exist on paper.

Static audit reports and self-assessments fail under regulatory examination when post-incident forensic evidence reveals unmitigated vulnerabilities.

Non-Delegable Vendor Risk

Outsourced IT & MSP Accountability

Regulators reject the defence that cyber security has been fully delegated to a managed service provider (MSP).

When a vendor compromise reaches your environment, the board remains accountable for failing to enforce controls over external access that were verified to work, not merely assumed to.
