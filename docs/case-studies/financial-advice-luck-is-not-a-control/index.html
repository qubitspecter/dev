Luck is not a control: finding the malware behind strange emails

Clients of a small financial advice practice began receiving strange emails about their accounts, and the firm's IT provider could not find the cause. When we looked, anyone could impersonate the firm by email, and remote access malware was already inside with ransomware being prepared. The attackers appear to have been held back by little more than a backup drive kept in a locked drawer. A month later the malware was gone, sign-in was phishing-resistant, and the firm had cyber insurance and a written security policy.

1. What happened

The firm paid its IT provider to improve its security. The provider delivered what many IT providers offer: a penetration test, some security product licences and a DMARC record. The firm reasonably believed it was covered. The provider was an IT provider, not a security provider, and the two are easy to confuse.

Then clients started receiving strange emails about changes to their accounts, asking for more information. The IT provider could not find the cause and passed the problem to Decoheron.

2. What we found

We started from the outside, using only information anyone can look up, and then looked inside. There was never just one problem:

  • The firm's DMARC policy was set to none (the setting that tells receiving mail systems to monitor and take no action), so anyone could send email pretending to be the firm and have it delivered. The firm's SPF record and mail providers were also visible in public DNS. The firm had not realised this was public, and its first reaction was to ask whether we had hacked it.
  • A spoofed email had been used to gain access to a real mailbox, which was then used to email clients about account changes and ask for additional information.
  • Remote access malware was already installed, along with signs of preparation for ransomware.
  • The antivirus had been disabled. When Microsoft Defender was switched back on, it detected the malware immediately.
  • Information may have been taken before we became involved.
  • The attackers appeared to know about the firm's offline backup. Backups went to a removable drive that was connected only while a backup ran, then disconnected and locked in a drawer. That habit may be the reason ransomware had not yet been deployed.

4. What changed

For the firm

The incident carried no ongoing cost, but that owed a lot to luck. A penetration test, antivirus, multi-factor authentication on one tool and a DMARC record had all been in place, and the environment was still compromised. The firm got through on a backup habit. It now has cyber insurance and a security programme that does not depend on luck.

For clients

The firm told its clients about the uplift and the insurance, and was able to explain what had changed. Email that fakes the firm's address is now refused by mail systems that check DMARC, so clients are far less likely to receive another message like the ones that started this.

For staff

Staff sign in with passkeys built into their devices, and only approved software runs. Few people hold admin rights, and the security policy is now part of how the firm operates day to day.
